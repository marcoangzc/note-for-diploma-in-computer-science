## 1. ICT and introduction (slides 4–8)

- IT boosts creativity and efficiency. Slide 4's productivity example: a company replaced several accountants when electronic payroll was introduced. **Ethical angle:** productivity gains can cost jobs; this is a trade-off, not a free benefit.
- **ICT** (information and communication technology) is described on slide 5 as integrating telephone and computer networking so data can be collected, used, controlled and retrieved easily.
- Importance of ICT (slides 6–8): builds critical thinking and creativity in students; stores and retrieves data; improves networking (Internet and intranet); boosts national economies and jobs; lets academics exchange ideas; supports e-learning and online libraries; essential to globalisation; keeps government records.

⚠️ The slide's definition is dated (a "single cabling device"). A modern short definition: technologies for handling information and communication (computers, networks, telephony, software).

## 2. Telemedicine and telehealth (slides 9–17)

**Telemedicine** = getting care from a provider through technology (video, web portals, email) instead of visiting the clinic. Slide 10's purposes: get a **diagnosis**, get **information on medical choices**, get a **prescription**.

### Three categories (slide 11)

| Category | Also called | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Immersive medicine** | "live telemedicine" | Doctor and patient interact in real time | Video consultation |
| **Remote patient monitoring** | — | Devices record data (blood pressure, blood sugar) and staff watch remotely | Nurse checking a diabetic's readings |
| **Store and forward** | — | Records or images are sent to another doctor or expert to review later | Sending a skin photo to a dermatologist |

**[extra]** Textbooks usually call the first category *synchronous* (real-time) and the third *asynchronous*. "Immersive" is unusual wording (it often suggests VR); use the slide's term in the exam.

### When to use / not use (slides 12–14)

- **Use for non-emergency** problems: minor issues, follow-ups, psychotherapy, tele-dermatology (moles, rashes), colds and flu, bug bites, sore throats, diarrhoea, pink eye; checking whether a cut is infected.
- **Not for emergencies**: heart attack, stroke, wounds or lacerations, broken bones needing X-rays, splints or casts. Anything needing urgent, hands-on care goes in person.

### Telemedicine vs telehealth (slide 15)

| | Telemedicine | Telehealth |
|---|---|---|
| Better statement | **Clinical** services delivered remotely (diagnosis, treatment, prescription) | **Broader**: includes telemedicine plus non-clinical activities such as appointment preparation, continuing medical education and specialist training |

⚠️ **Slide 15 contradicts slides 12 and 14.** Its telemedicine cell says patients get medication without a visit "or visit their clinic for emergency care", yet slides 12 and 14 say telemedicine is for non-emergencies only. Remember: *telemedicine = remote clinical care, non-emergency; telehealth = telemedicine + non-clinical health services.*

### Advantages (slide 16)

| Advantage | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Comfort and convenience | No travel; easier scheduling |
| Control of infectious illness | Pre-screening reduces exposure of others |
| Better assessment | Doctor sees the home environment (e.g. allergy sources, mental-health cues) |
| Family connections | A relative can join, ask questions, take notes |

Slide 17: telemedicine has been especially useful in mental health (psychiatrist access "at the touch of a button", lower cost). ⚠️ "More beneficial than in any other field" is a strong claim with no evidence on the slide; quote it as the slide does.

## 3. Mobility (slides 18–21)

**In this topic "mobility" means mobile technology in health care** (smart devices and apps), not transport.

- Changes the patient/provider relationship; patients see results online 24/7; providers keep records in the cloud instead of paper.
- Enables telemedicine: live streaming of medical data, high-fidelity audio on handheld devices, IoT medical devices sharing information.
- Advantages: improved efficiency; better patient experience in and out of hospital; better contact and coordination between patients and professionals; patient tracking by mobile device is expected to save large costs.

⚠️ **The chapter uses "mobility" in two senses.** Slides 18–21 = mobile health. Slides 22–30 ("Social Equity in Telehealth & Mobility") = **smart mobility**, i.e. transport services such as ride-hailing. When answering, state which meaning you are using.

## 4. Social equity in telehealth and mobility (slides 22–30)

**Social equity** = everyone benefits fairly from technology-enabled health care and transport services regardless of income, age, location, disability or digital skills.

### Challenges

| Challenge | Cause and effect |
|---|---|
| **Digital divide** | Rural and low-income people lack connectivity and devices → less access |
| **Digital literacy** | Elderly or inexperienced users struggle with apps |
| **Accessibility** | Platforms not designed for people with disabilities |
| **Result** | Technology meant to convenience some may unintentionally exclude others |

### Four ways to promote equity (slides 27–30)

| Approach | Slide example |
|---|---|
| **Inclusive design** | Telehealth apps with screen readers and multilingual support; simple mobility apps for elderly users |
| **Affordable access** | Subsidised internet or low-cost data plans; affordable alternative transport for low-income commuters |
| **Fair algorithms** | Do not prioritise tech-savvy users in appointment scheduling; ride-hailing must not consistently raise prices in lower-income areas |
| **Digital education** | Community training for elderly patients; workshops on mobility apps and digital payments |

Memory aid: **I-A-F-E** ("Inclusive, Affordable, Fair, Educated").

## 5. Electronic health records (slides 31–35)

**EHR** = a computerised patient record that is real-time and patient-centred and available securely to authorised users. It can hold medical and prescription history, diagnoses, recovery schedules, vaccination dates, allergies, radiology images and test results, and gives clinicians evidence-based resources and streamlined workflow.

**EMR vs EHR [extra]:** an **EMR** (electronic medical record) is the digital version of a chart inside one practice; an **EHR** is designed to be shared across organisations. The slides use EMR on slide 34 without defining it.

### Benefits (slides 33–35)

| Benefit | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Better quality of care** | Easy access to information; analytics spot patterns, forecast diagnoses and suggest treatments; patients view lab results and message doctors |
| **Interoperability** (slide label: "willingness to communicate with one another") | Systems exchange data; vital for chronic illness such as diabetes and for home care |
| **Increased productivity** | More precise care, shorter waits, more patients treated |
| Also (slide 35) | More patient participation, better coordination, better diagnostics and outcomes, cost savings |

**Ethics link:** because EHRs are electronic health data, they fall under health-privacy laws (HIPAA, Chapter 3) and Malaysia's PDPA.

## 6. Big data and cloud computing (slides 36–46)

**Big data** = the huge volume of data generated from many sources; used for analytics and prediction, e.g. early identification of disease outbreaks. **[extra]** Commonly described by the "Vs": volume, velocity, variety (also veracity, value).

### Cloud computing (slides 39–41)

Cloud is suited to big data because it offers flexibility, parallel processing, scalability and resource virtualisation.

Slide 40 characteristics: uses Internet technologies to offer **elastic** services (resources acquired dynamically for variable workloads); resources are **metered** (pay only for what you use); **user convenience** (provider handles maintenance and security).

**Slide 41's summary diagram (figure the text misses):**

| Box | Contents |
|---|---|
| Delivery models | SaaS (Software), PaaS (Platform), IaaS (Infrastructure), DBaaS (Database as a Service) |
| Deployment models | Public, private, community, hybrid cloud |
| Defining attributes | Massive infrastructure, utility computing (pay-per-usage), accessible via the Internet, elasticity |
| Infrastructure | Distributed infrastructure, resource virtualisation, autonomous systems |
| Resources | Compute and storage servers, networks, services, applications |

**[extra]** Most standard definitions list three delivery models (SaaS, PaaS, IaaS); DBaaS is an added type here.

### How cloud affects health care (slides 42–46)

Cost-cutting (no upfront hardware) · interoperability (linking data from many sources, sharing with insurers and payment systems) · links to analytics and AI (personalised treatment plans) · patient data possession (patient engagement, quick storage and retrieval of records and images, automatic backups) · telemedicine skills (remote access, post-hospital care, medication adherence).

⚠️ **Slide 45 is loosely worded.** "Decentralizing data" and "reduced data redundancy" sit oddly with automatic backups (which *add* copies) and with cloud usually *centralising* data. Read it as: patients get easier access to their records, with fewer inconsistent duplicate records, "despite ongoing security concerns". Slide 39 also claims cloud offers "security" — security is shared between provider and customer, and cloud is not automatically safer.

## 7. Privacy in digital technology (slides 47–52)

**Privacy** concerns the collection and use of data about individuals. Three primary issues (slide 48):

| Issue | Question |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Are those who collect data responsible for making it correct? |
| **Property** | Who owns the data? |
| **Access** | Who controls who may use the data? |

Why it matters: almost every event is recorded (a phone call log can include who, when, where and even content); the stored mass is **big data**; large organisations compile data (credit-card companies, supermarket scanners, banks, search engines, social networks). Slide 50's "federal government has over 2,000 databases" is a US figure from the textbook; do not treat it as universal.

Searchable data lets someone assemble your **digital footprint**, and **information resellers / brokers** collect and sell such data. Issues (slide 52): collecting public but personally identifying information; spreading information without consent; spreading inaccurate information.

## 8. Internet and the web (slides 53–59)

**IP address.** Each device on the Internet has a number used to trace activity to its origin for investigating unauthorised access or copyright sharing. ⚠️ **[extra]** In practice one IP can be shared by many devices (home routers, workplaces) and can be hidden by a VPN, so an IP address is a clue, not proof of who did something.

**Deep web vs dark web (slide 54).**

| | Deep web | Dark web |
|---|---|---|
| Definition [extra, general knowledge] | Any content not indexed by standard search engines (e.g. web-mail, banking pages, subscription databases) | A small part of the deep web reached with special software that hides the user's IP address |
| Slide's wording | "Hidden from standard search engines, allow communication in a secure and anonymous manner" | "Special software hides IP and makes it nearly impossible to identify users" |

⚠️ The slide's description of the deep web fits the dark web better. The slide also mentions crime (drugs, wildlife trafficking) as the reason anonymity attracts criminals. **[extra]** Anonymity also protects journalists and whistleblowers.

**What the browser stores (slide 55):** **history** files (addresses of recent sites) and **temporary Internet files / cache** (page content saved to redisplay quickly).

### Cookies (slides 56–58)

| | First-party cookie | Third-party cookie |
|---|---|---|
| Created by | The site you are visiting | Usually an advertising company affiliated with the site |
| Purpose | Session info, preferences, personalisation (e.g. remembering your shopping cart) | Track your activity across sites (tracking cookies) |
| Slide example | Cart items remembered after you leave | Three car sites then a search engine → you see a car advertisement |

⚠️ Slide 56 calls cookies "harmless" but a "potential privacy risk": they are plain data files, not programs, but they can store preferences and browsing habits.

**Privacy mode (slide 59).** Deleting history/cache and using modes like Chrome's Incognito or Safari's Private Browsing stops the browser recording activity **on your hard disk**. ⚠️ **[extra]** It does *not* hide what you do from the websites you visit, your employer or school network, or your Internet provider.

## 9. Online identity (slides 60–61)

**Online identity** = information people voluntarily post about themselves. Posts stay searchable and archived for anyone. Examples on slide 61: people lost jobs over posts (a teacher with offensive language/photos; a CFO discussing corporate dealings); graduates refused jobs because of Facebook posts.

Reflection question posed: *How would you feel if information you posted kept you from getting a job?* Suggested answer: it shows that "private among friends" is an illusion online.

## 10. Facial recognition and privacy rights (slides 62–70)

**Biometric facial recognition** = digital comparison of the arrangement of facial features by an algorithm.

### The five steps (slides 63–66, diagram on slide 66)

| # | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Capture** | Take a digital photograph |
| 2 | **Processing** | Scale and align the face to a baseline position |
| 3 | **Modelling** | Quantify features into a contour map, converted to a **digital template** |
| 4 | **Comparison** | Compare pairs of templates and produce a numerical score (probability they are the same person) |
| 5 | **Decision making** | Decide match or no match |

**Verification (slide 65)** = one-to-one matching: a live face compared with the template stored in an ID document (e.g. passport at border control). **[extra]** **Identification** is one-to-many: searching a face against a whole database, which is where privacy concerns are usually greater.

### Limitations (slide 67)

Accuracy is lower than fingerprints or DNA; how often facial features occur in the population is unknown; image quality matters; database size affects accuracy; faces change over time causing false positives or negatives. **[extra]** Studies also find accuracy can differ across demographic groups.

### Privacy rights (slides 68–70)

Privacy is a right held against other persons, the state and organisations with respect to:
(a) **informational** privacy: others possessing information (including facial images) about you, e.g. biometric databases;
(b) **observational** privacy: others observing you, your movements and relationships.
Facial recognition involves *both*. The core conflict is **security vs individual privacy**.

## 11. Fingerprint and applied ethics (slides 71–74)

**Biometric identification** = measuring physical aspects of the body (skin patterns, blood-vessel patterns, genetic code, facial appearance). Reasons to use it (slide 72): unique between individuals; unchanging; can be digitised by an algorithm; can be stored and searched automatically; contrasts with keys, ID cards, passwords.

**Fingerprint identification (slide 73, figure):** based on ridge patterns classified as **arches, loops and whorls**; the centre of a pattern is the **core**; points of deviation are **deltas**. Used beyond law enforcement, first in passports and border control together with facial recognition (slide 74).

⚠️ **Slide 72 vs slide 67.** Slide 72 says biometrics are "unchanging over time" while slide 67 says faces change over time. Read slide 72 as "relatively stable". **[extra]** A key ethical difference from passwords: you cannot replace a stolen fingerprint.

**[verified]** Malaysia's amended PDPA now names biometric data as a type of **sensitive personal data** (from April 2025).

## 12–13. Smartphone applications and social media (slides 75–85)

**Smartphone (slides 75–76).** Fingerprint and face unlock are widely used; because they are secure, possession of a person's registered phone acts as a **proxy for their identity or location**. The phone is used for tap-and-pay, QR-code check-in, and access to social media and accounts, making it an "extension of the physical self".

**Social media (slides 77–84).** Biometrics that unlock the phone also unlock social media accounts, so they become identity indicators online. Social media involves interaction and non-hierarchical collaboration: users post content, create profiles, comment/like, network.

Concerns: tech firms hold vast activity data; presentation of information shapes views; less central control lets falsehoods spread; anonymous harassment (slide 81: online harassment targets race, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation and affects health, safety and productivity); misinformation spread by **botnets** (e.g. about COVID-19); targeted advertising to influence elections.

⚠️ **Dated example (slide 77).** Facebook used face recognition for photo tagging, but it ended tag suggestions in 2019 and, in November 2021, announced it was shutting down its face-recognition system and deleting the face templates of over a billion users. Use it as a *case study*, not a current feature. **[verified]**

### The identity verification debate (slides 80–85)

| For verifying identity (passport/licence) | Against |
|---|---|
| Deter harassment, hate speech and disinformation; make abuse easier to investigate and prosecute; like ID needed to drive or own a firearm | Data security: identity documents held by large tech firms with huge existing data are a target for organised crime; raises risk |

⚠️ **Update for Malaysia (slide 84).** The slide says laws requiring compulsory identity verification "have not been introduced". Malaysia's Online Safety Act 2025 took effect on 1 January 2026, and reports say the Child Protection Code (from **1 June 2026**) requires large platforms to prevent under-16s from registering and to check age against government records such as MyKad or passport. This is age verification rather than full "real-name" identity, and some of my sources are vendor blogs, so check MCMC's own guidance before quoting details. **[verified with caveat]** For the exam, give the slide's argument, then this update as an example of the debate becoming real.

## 14. Major laws on privacy (slide 86)

US examples: **Gramm-Leach-Bliley** (personal financial information), **HIPAA** (medical records), **FERPA** (education records). The slide adds that most information collected by private organisations is **not covered** by existing laws, and that companies and lawmakers will respond as public concern grows.

⚠️ That statement describes the US. In Malaysia the PDPA covers personal data in commercial transactions (Chapter 3).

## 15. Green IT (slides 87–89)

**Green IT** = designing, using and managing IT systems in an environmentally sustainable way, reducing harm at every stage: manufacturing, operation (computing, storage, networks) and disposal (e-waste).

| Importance | Challenges |
|---|---|
| Environmental sustainability (ICT contributes significantly to carbon emissions) | High initial investment |
| Growing e-waste (rapid upgrades; toxic impact if disposed improperly) | Rapid obsolescence increases e-waste |
| High energy consumption (data centres, cloud, networks) | Lack of awareness: performance is prioritised over sustainability |
| | Poor recycling systems in many countries |

(Compare Chapter 4 green procurement.)

## Answers

**A1.** (c). (a) is live; (b) remote monitoring.
**A2.** (b) Third-party.
**A3.** (b). One-to-one verification (slide 65).
**A4.** (d). Accuracy, property, access.
**A5.** (c) Fair algorithms (slide 29).

**B1.** Telemedicine = clinical services delivered remotely; telehealth = telemedicine plus non-clinical services (education, training, appointment preparation). Slide 15's telemedicine cell mentions emergency care, but slides 12 and 14 say telemedicine is suitable only for non-emergency situations.
**B2.** Informational: an organisation holds your facial image in a biometric database. Observational: cameras watch your movements and relationships. Facial recognition involves both.
**B3.** For: deters anonymous harassment, hate speech and disinformation; makes abuse easier to investigate. Against: identity documents held by large companies become targets for criminals; increases data risk and reduces privacy/anonymity.

**C1.** (b), (c), (e), (a), (d): capture, processing, modelling, comparison, decision.
**C2.** Inclusive design: app with screen reader and local language. Affordable access: subsidised data plan or free clinic Wi-Fi. Fair algorithms: appointment system that does not favour app-savvy users. Digital education: community training sessions for elderly patients.
**C3.** The ad agency's third-party cookie recorded your visits to the cooking sites and identifies you at the news site, so it shows related ads.

**D1.** Slide 67 says faces change over time and can give false matches, so "unchanging" is only relative. Privacy issue: unlike passwords, biometrics cannot be replaced if stolen **[extra]**, and biometric databases raise informational privacy risk.
**D2.** Cloud gives scalable storage and analytics for research, cost savings and easy data sharing. But health data is highly sensitive; a breach could expose many patients. Security is a shared responsibility between provider and customer. Laws such as PDPA and HIPAA and strong access controls are needed.
