# AMIS1012 — Chapter 4: Ethics of IT Organisation

## 1. Organisational ethics (slides 4–7)

Slide 4 gives two definitions: how an organisation reacts to the **external** world, and the rules and standards that determine how **employees** act at work. Use both: ethics faces outwards (customers, society) and inwards (staff conduct).

The behaviours listed (slides 4–7), grouped:

| Group | Rule |
|---|---|
| Honesty with data | Tampering with data is dishonest and unprofessional |
| Confidentiality | Do not disclose confidential information to third parties, family or friends; classified details must never be leaked |
| Fairness | Total justice in monetary transactions; no discrimination by identity, appearance, age, family history; judge employees purely on their work |
| Employee treatment | Pay people for their contributions; credit late-night work; pay salaries, incentives and arrears on time |
| Safety | Do not place people in dangerous situations |
| Honesty with customers | No false claims; advertising must be clear; do not promise what the business can't keep |

⚠️ **Slide 6 wording.** It says female employees must be treated with dignity and should not be expected to work late. The principle the slide supports (fairness, no discrimination) applies to **all** employees. Singling out one group contradicts slide 5's non-discrimination rule, so give the general principle in the exam.

## 2. Rules and laws of IT organisations (slides 8–13)

### 2.1 United States (slides 8–11)

Slide 8 says the US electronic protection framework is "probably the world's largest, most comprehensive, and effective". Treat this as opinion: US law is a patchwork by sector, whereas GDPR (Ch3) is one general law.

| Act (slide) | Applies to | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| HIPAA 1996 | Healthcare organisations | Safeguard health data |
| Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act 1999 | Financial institutions | Safeguard customer financial data |
| FISMA (slide: "included in the Homeland Security Act of 2002") | Government departments | Information security in federal agencies |
| Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act (**CISA**) | Government and companies | Cyber-threat information sharing |
| "2014 Cybersecurity Improvement Act" | Public–private | Voluntary collaboration, research, workforce, awareness |
| "2015 Federal Exchange Data Breach Disclosure Act" | Health insurance exchanges | Notify affected customers of breaches within 60 days of discovery |

Slide 10 explains that HIPAA, GLBA and FISMA "require" safeguards but only a **"fair" degree** of security, so they are not foolproof.

⚠️ **Corrections found by checking:**

- **CISA is voluntary, not mandatory.** Slide 11 says the law "mandates" that government and companies share Internet-traffic information. The Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 sets up a framework for **voluntary** sharing of cyber-threat indicators, with liability protections and no duty to share. It also had a sunset date: it lapsed in September 2025, was temporarily extended, and was scheduled to expire on **30 September 2026** unless extended again. Check its current status.
- **Name of the 2014 Act.** The slide's "Cybersecurity Improvement Act" is the **Cybersecurity Enhancement Act of 2014** (Public Law 113-274), described as an ongoing voluntary public–private partnership for cybersecurity, research, workforce development and awareness.
- **Not checked [extra]:** the exact name and status of the "2015 Federal Exchange Data Breach" law (the slide calls it both a "bill" and an "act") and whether FISMA sits in the Homeland Security Act or the E-Government Act. Learn the slide's version for the exam.

### 2.2 Other countries (slide 12)

| Country | Law | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Australia and New Zealand | Freedom of Information (FOI) laws | Introduced as early as 1982, later updated |
| China | **PIPL**, in effect **1 November 2021** | China's first comprehensive data protection law; processing must follow principles of legality, justice (propriety), integrity, minimum necessity, openness and transparency; purposes must be explicit and reasonable |

### 2.3 Malaysia (slide 13): Whistleblower Protection Act 2010 (Act 711)

Purpose: promote disclosure of corruption or other misconduct and protect whistleblowers from acts prejudicial to them. Four protections on the slide:

1. Identity of the informant and the information stays confidential, even during trial.
2. No civil, criminal or disciplinary liability for the disclosure.
3. Protection from acts prejudicial to the outcome of the disclosure.
4. Protection extends to people connected to the informant.

**[extra, verified]** The Act came into force on **15 December 2010**. Disclosures go to an **enforcement agency** (e.g. police, MACC); protection is lost if the person goes public before reporting to an agency (see the correction under slide 20 below).

## 3. Whistle blowing and corporate accountability (slides 14–26)

**Whistle blowing** = a worker discloses facts about corruption or wrongdoing ("making a disclosure", "blowing the whistle").

To be shielded by whistleblowing law (slide 14) the worker reasonably believes:
1. they act in the best interests of the group (public interest), and
2. the disclosure indicates actual, present or probable misconduct in one of six categories (slide 15): illegal violations (e.g. fraud); failure to comply with a lawful duty; miscarriages of justice; endangering someone's health and welfare; environmental harm; covering up any of these.

⚠️ **Slides 14–16 are describing UK law.** The "Employment Protection Act of 1996 … as amended by the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998" is really the UK **Employment Rights Act 1996** as amended by PIDA 1998, and the "employment tribunal" is the UK forum. The six categories match UK law's list. For Malaysia, the relevant law is Act 711 (slide 13). If asked, say which country you are describing.

### The 8-step whistleblowing process (slides 17–20)

| Step | Action | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Determine the severity** | Have clear evidence that the wrongdoing threatens the public interest |
| 2 | **Begin documentation** | Record incidents, facts and your perceptions |
| 3 | **Try to fix internally** | Written summary to relevant supervisors; focus on the truth and impact on others |
| 4 | **Consider the next level inside** | The first try often fails; escalating shows you did everything necessary |
| 5 | **Consider the implications** | Legal fees; the company has more money and lawyers |
| 6 | **Develop an action plan with experienced help** | Get legal advice from a qualified attorney |
| 7 | **Put the plan into action** | Work with legal counsel |
| 8 | **Accept the consequences** | Expect retaliation: discredited, harassed, set up, demoted or fired over small issues |

Memory aid: **S-D-I-N-C-P-A-C** → "Some Diligent Insiders Need Careful Planning And Courage".

⚠️ **Slide 20's advice.** It says the best way for someone wanting to stay confidential is to "leak information to the press anonymously". That conflicts with Malaysian law: under Act 711, whistleblowers who go public *before* reporting to an enforcement agency are not covered by the Act's protection, and Malaysian ministers have confirmed this in Parliament. Answer the exam as the slide does if asked, but know the legal position.

### Non-whistleblowing situations (slides 21–24)

A non-whistleblowing situation is an internal matter that does not involve illegal, unethical or dangerous conduct by the company and can be resolved through **standard company procedures**.

| Case | Why it is not whistleblowing |
|---|---|
| **Emma** asks to move from evening to morning shifts | Personal request about work conditions; routine |
| **Carlos** reports a malfunctioning forklift to the supervisor and maintenance | No wrongdoing or cover-up; reported through normal channels and fixed promptly |
| **Lena** disputes a payroll error on her overtime | Affects her personally; a mistake, not misconduct; payroll corrected |

⚠️ **Slide 23 error.** Its "why it is not whistleblowing" text is copied from Emma's slide ("a personal request…"). It does not fit Carlos, whose report is about safety, not a personal request. The right reason: a safety fault reported through the normal process and repaired. It *would become* a whistleblowing situation if the company kept ignoring warnings or hid the fault (health and safety endangered, cover-up).

### Corporate accountability (slides 25–26)

The company must take responsibility for its decisions and their effects on employees, customers, investors and society, and be open about performance. Five principles with the slides' examples:

| Principle | Example |
|---|---|
| **Transparency** | Publishes real financial reports; does not hide losses |
| **Responsibility** | Recalls a defective product and apologises instead of blaming customers |
| **Compliance** | An IT company follows data protection law such as the PDPA |
| **Stakeholder protection** | Factory provides protective equipment and safe conditions |
| **Board oversight** | Board investigates managers suspected of fraud |

## 4. Employers' responsibilities (slides 27–29)

- Create an inclusive, clear, healthy atmosphere where staff feel safe to speak up.
- Legislation may not *require* a whistleblowing programme, but having one shows willingness to listen.
- Clear rules for handling whistleblowers show the organisation values information brought to management.
- Staff often first notice fraud; their findings can protect the organisation's credibility and save people from harm.
- Without a transparent atmosphere, workers fear **retaliation** and believe **nothing will be done** — the two key obstacles.

## 5. ICT code of conduct (slides 30–34)

A **code of conduct** = rules and guides that set out expectations of a person or organisation; an **ICT code** covers ethical, efficient and secure use of computers and the Internet.

| # | Element | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fair and honest business | Follow best business practice |
| 2 | Legal compliance | Follow laws of the countries of operation, including health, safety and environment |
| 3 | Data security | Protect sensitive information; strong passwords; never share logins; report breaches or vulnerabilities immediately |
| 4 | Integrity | Respect others; no harassment, bullying or digital discrimination; respect privacy |
| 5 | Usage | Use resources only for authorised purposes; no unauthorised access, modification or disclosure; no personal gain or illegal use |
| 6 | Software and licensing | Only licensed software; no unauthorised installs |
| 7 | Monitoring and compliance | The organisation may monitor use; users must understand and follow laws and policies |

Note the link: element 3's "never share logins" is the exact user habit slide 9 of Chapter 2 identifies as a vulnerability.

## 6. Green procurement policy (slides 35–37)

**Green procurement** = choosing IT hardware, software and digital services that reduce environmental harm over their **whole life cycle** (production, energy use, maintenance, disposal).

| Principle | Example on the slide |
|---|---|
| Environmental sustainability | Low-power laptops instead of high-energy desktops |
| Life cycle thinking | Send old computers to certified e-waste recycling |
| Ethical supplier selection | Buy from vendors that avoid conflict minerals |
| Energy and resource efficiency | Virtualisation to reduce the number of physical servers |
| Regulatory and ethical compliance | Follow standards such as ISO 14001 |

⚠️ **Simplified (slide 35).** "Cloud services powered by renewable energy instead of high-energy local servers" is true only for a specific green provider; moving to the cloud is not automatically greener.

**C1.** Priya finds her manager altering safety test results. Put the 8 steps in order with one action each.

## Answers

**A1.** (b) Act 711.
**A2.** (b).
**A3.** (b). The others involve concealment or wrongdoing.
**A4.** (b) Compliance.
**A5.** (b) Life cycle thinking (recycling at end of life).

**B1.** They must reasonably believe (1) they are acting in the best interests of the group/public, and (2) the disclosure indicates actual, present or probable misconduct in a protected category.
**B2.** Fear of retribution, and belief that nothing will be done.
**B3.** Any four of: fair business; legal compliance; data security; integrity; usage; software and licensing; monitoring and compliance.

**C1.** (1) Determine severity: confirm altered results endanger the public. (2) Document dates, results, copies. (3) Report in writing to the supervisor or manager's superior. (4) Escalate inside the company if ignored. (5) Consider the costs and risks. (6) Get legal advice/action plan. (7) Carry out the plan (report to the proper authority). (8) Be ready for retaliation and use the plan and solicitor.
**C2.** (i) Not: routine equipment issue. (ii) Whistleblowing: fraud and concealment. (iii) Not: personal payroll matter.
**C3.** HIPAA → healthcare; GLBA → financial institutions; FISMA → government departments.

**D1.** Under Act 711, protection depends on reporting to an enforcement agency first; leaking to the press first can lose that protection and expose the person to action.
**D2.** If the company repeatedly ignored warnings or hid the fault, the situation would involve endangering health and safety and a cover-up, so a disclosure would fall into a protected category.
