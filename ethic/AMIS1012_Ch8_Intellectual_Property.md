# AMIS1012 — Chapter 8: Intellectual Property

> The slides name the four types of IP but leave out key distinctions (what each type protects, how long, whether you register) and contain a few wrong or garbled definitions (patent as a "license", cybersquatting returning a domain to the "copyright owner", a patent term that was changed in 2000). ## 0. One-sentence overview

Intellectual property (IP) is the set of legal rights over creations of the mind; the chapter explains the four main types (patents, trademarks, copyright, trade secrets) under Malaysian law and six related issues (plagiarism, reverse engineering, open source, competitive intelligence, trademark infringement, cybersquatting).

## 1. Introduction (slides 4–7)

- **IP** = creations of the mind: inventions, literary and artistic works, and symbols, names, images and designs used in commerce.
- **IPR (Intellectual Property Rights)** = rights arising from the results of intellectual activity that have economic benefit. The most popular IPRs: copyrights, patents, trade secrets and trademarks.
- IP is intangible, but can be worth more than physical assets, so companies guard it and seek income from it.
- What IPR give the owner: the right to benefit fully from the creation; to stop others using it without permission; to sue and demand that infringers **stop and pay compensation** for losses. (Slide 7's wording "stop compensating him" is garbled; the meaning is *stop and compensate*.)

## 2. The four types compared

| | **Patent** | **Trademark** | **Copyright** | **Trade secret** (confidential information) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protects | A new invention (product or process) | A sign that distinguishes goods or services of one business from another | Original works expressed in material form (books, music, art, software, films) | Confidential information that gives a business a competitive edge |
| Malaysian law | Patents Act 1983 and Patents Regulations 1986 | Trademarks Act 2019 | Copyright Act 1987 (amended, incl. 2012 and 2022) | No specific statute; contract, common law and other laws |
| Registration | Yes (application to the Malaysian IP office) | Yes (registration) | Automatic **[extra]**; voluntary notification exists | **Not registrable** in Malaysia |
| Duration | **20 years from filing date** **[verified]** | 10 years, renewable every 10 years **[verified]** | Life of the author + **50 years** (literary, musical, artistic works) | As long as it stays secret |
| Requirements | New, involves an inventive step, industrially applicable | Distinctive (see spectrum) | Original, in physical form | Confidential and commercially valuable |

## 3. Patents (slides 9–10)

**Slide definition:** a patent enables an inventor to commercialise a product for a fixed period to the exclusion of others.
⚠️ Slide 9 calls a patent "a license given to an author". A patent is an **exclusive right granted to an inventor** (not an author and not a licence, though the owner can license it to others). It also says "any fresh and beneficial thing is called innovation" — a patent needs more than being new and useful (see the three tests below).

**Patentable if (slide 10):** it is **new**, involves an **inventive step**, and is **industrially applicable**. An application can be made directly in Malaysia; registration is effective throughout Malaysia. The owner may exploit the invention, assign or transmit the patent and conclude licence contracts.

⚠️ **Term error.** Slide 10 says a patent expires **15 years after grant**. Malaysia's Patents Act was revised in 2000 to extend the term from 15 to 20 years; today a patent lasts **20 years from the filing date** (subject to annual fees). Use 20 years from filing. **[verified]**
**[extra, verified]** *Utility innovations* (minor inventions needing novelty but not an inventive step) are protected for 10 years from filing, extendable twice by 5 years each (10 + 5 + 5).

## 4. Trademarks (slides 11–16)

**Trademark** = a sign capable of distinguishing one enterprise's goods or services from another's. The **Trademarks Act 2019** provides for registration of trademarks and implements relevant treaties. Search thoroughly before registering.

### Distinctiveness spectrum (slides 12–15)

Five categories, from weakest to strongest protection:

| Category | Meaning | Slide 13 example | Figure examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | Common term for the product; very hard or impossible to register or enforce | CLOCK | Slide 15: a clock; slide 14: Aspirin, Xerox |
| **Descriptive** | Directly describes the goods/services | COLD AND CREAMY (ice cream) | Singapore Airlines, Café Coffee Day; Sharp |
| **Suggestive** | Hints at a quality without describing it directly | SWEETARTS | Infosys, Burger King; Airbus, FedEx |
| **Arbitrary** | Real word applied to an unrelated product | BLACKBERRY (phones) | Apple, Starbucks; Amazon |
| **Fanciful / Coined** | Invented word with no other meaning; easiest to register and enforce | KODAK | Kodak, Adidas; Exxon |

Memory aid: **G-D-S-A-F** — "Good Designs Start As Fancy".

⚠️ **Figure confusion.** The two figures use different example brands, and slide 14 places **Aspirin and Xerox** under "generic". These marks were once distinctive but became the everyday word for the product (**genericide**), which is different from a word that was generic from the start like CLOCK for clocks. In the exam use the slide 13 examples.

**Slide 16** is a news article (New Straits Times, 26 April 2024) headlined that trademark and copyright infringement still occur despite wider awareness of IP rights; use it as evidence that infringement remains common.

## 5. Copyright and related rights (slides 17–22)

**Copyright** = the legal right of an author, artist or creator to commercially exploit their original work that has been expressed physically, and to stop others reproducing or reusing it without consent.

**[extra]** Copyright protects the *expression*, not the underlying *idea*; and in Malaysia copyright arises automatically when the work is created, while registration of copyright is not required (a voluntary notification system exists).

**Copyright Act 1987 (slides 18–19):** the primary Malaysian law; sets out rights and obligations of creators and owners and remedies for infringement; drafted on international standards including the **Berne Convention**; the consolidated version includes amendments up to the Copyright (Amendment) Act 2012 (Act A1420).

**Copyright (Amendment) Act 2022 (slide 20):** aims to comply with international standards and agreements (RCEP, the Marrakesh Treaty and CPTPP) and strengthens enforcement in the digital environment, including offences involving **streaming technology**. **[verified]** The 2022 amendment covered collective management organisations, print-disability access (Marrakesh), voluntary notification, streaming technology and enforcement powers; it did not change the copyright term.

### Duration (slide 21)

| Place | Term for literary, musical and artistic works |
|---|---|
| Malaysia | Life of the creator + **50 years** after death **[verified]** |
| Singapore, US, EU (per the slide) | Life + **70 years** |

After expiry the work enters the **public domain**.

**Worked example:** an author dies in 2000. Under the slide's Malaysian rule (life + 50 years) the work stays protected until about 2050; under the life + 70 years rule the slide gives for the EU it would last until about 2070. (The exact cut-off date depends on how the Act counts from the year of death, which I did not check.)

**Advantages (slide 22):** encourages imagination and innovation by rewarding creators; helps businesses produce, grow and distribute works globally, supporting cultural and economic development.

## 6. Trade secrets (slides 23–27)

**Trade secrets** are IP rights over confidential information that gives a competitive advantage and has not been disclosed; they may be licensed or sold. Examples: technical information (research and engineering data, drawings, process sheets, software source code) and commercial information (marketing strategy, production data, records of commercial activity).

- In Malaysia "trade secret" is interchangeable with **"confidential information"**, the term recognised in law.
- **Cannot be registered** but you can take legal action if it is exploited.
- **No specific trade-secret statute**; protection comes from contract law, common law and other statutes. Slide 25's examples: the Competition Act 2010 (misuse in certain cases) and the Penal Code (criminal breach of trust).
- **Protective measures (slide 27):** *internal*: strengthen employee agreements, implement access controls, address everyday practices; *external*: **non-disclosure agreements (NDAs)**.

⚠️ **[unverified]** I could not confirm that the Competition Act 2010 addresses misuse of trade secrets; treat slide 25 as the slide's claim. Contract and breach-of-confidence law are the main routes.

**Compare:** a patent gives 20 years of exclusivity in exchange for public disclosure; a trade secret can last forever but is lost once it is disclosed (or lawfully discovered).

## 7. Issues related to IP (slides 28–45)

### 7.1 Plagiarism (slides 29–30)

**Plagiarism** = stealing someone else's thoughts or words and claiming them as your own, e.g. cut-and-paste without attribution or quotation marks. It occurs at all levels of education and in literature, music, journalism and software development. Causes on the slide: students think electronic material is free, pressure to get grades, tiredness or lack of time.

⚠️ **Plagiarism ≠ copyright infringement [extra].** Plagiarism is an *ethical/academic* wrong (passing off others' work as yours). Copyright infringement is a *legal* wrong (using a protected work without permission). You can plagiarise a public-domain work (no copyright) and you can infringe copyright while crediting the author.

### 7.2 Reverse engineering (slides 31–33)

**Reverse engineering** = examining a commercially available product to find its structure, components and technology by taking it apart (for software: examining compiled code). A rival can discover production and design secrets to use in its own inventions.

Is it illegal in Malaysia? Slide 33: **no specific law directly prohibits it**, but it can be indirectly restricted by the **Copyright Act 1987, Patents Act 1983, trade secret law and EULAs** (end-user licence agreements).

### 7.3 Open source code (slides 34–36)

| | Open source | Closed source |
|---|---|---|
| Source code | Publicly available; anyone can access, modify and redistribute | Not disclosed, or disclosed only under strict confidentiality |

Benefits: a robust resource for developers and companies, reviewed by many people.
**Licence terms matter (slide 36):** before using open-source code, research the license terms; if you want to keep your own software proprietary and secret, you may need to ask the owner whether a proprietary licence (for royalties) can be granted.

⚠️ **Simplifications.** (1) Slide 35 says the Chrome interface and Netflix's channels are "based on open-source technologies". **[extra]** Chrome is built on the open-source Chromium project, but Chrome itself is a Google product; do not call Chrome "open source". (2) Open source is not "public domain": licences attach conditions, and they differ (**[extra]** *permissive* licences such as MIT allow use in proprietary software with credit; *copyleft* licences such as GPL require you to release derived code under the same terms). (3) Open source is not the same as free of charge.

### 7.4 Competitive intelligence (slides 37–38)

**Competitive intelligence (CI)** = discovering, analysing and gathering patent and market data about an industry and its competitors. Purposes: see industry progress, competitors' strengths, weaknesses and IP; learn their strategies, products and next moves; identify risks and opportunities. In the IPR field it supports decisions on market share, technology evaluation, licences and monitoring rivals. Applying for IPR takes time and money, which is part of why CI is useful.

**[extra] Ethical line (the slides don't state it):** CI is ethical when it uses **public** sources (patent databases, published reports, websites). It becomes unethical or illegal when it uses deception, bribery, hacking or stolen confidential information (industrial espionage).

### 7.5 Trademark infringement (slide 39)

**Trademark infringement** = illegal use of a trademark or service mark on goods or services, which can cause confusion or deception about the true source. The owner can sue; a court **injunction** can prohibit the misuse and the owner may receive monetary compensation. ("A criminal" on the slide is loose wording: the infringer is usually a civil defendant, though serious counterfeiting can also be an offence.)

### 7.6 Cybersquatting (slides 40–45)

**Cybersquatting** = registering domain names that are identical or close to a business name or trademark to profit from the brand's goodwill or to **resell** the domain, or for criminal uses such as phishing sites, frauds and bogus surveys.

⚠️ **Slide 41 error.** It says the aim is to transfer the domain back to the **"copyright owner"**. It should be the **trademark owner**, and the squatter's aim is usually to **sell** it to them for a profit.

**Four types (slide 42 figure; details slides 43–45):**

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity theft** | Register a domain that looks identical to a real business's to impersonate it and lure users to a phishing site | A fake bank login site |
| **Name jacking** | Register a false site/profile using a famous person's name | Fake celebrity domain or social media profile |
| **Typosquatting** | Register misspelled versions of popular names | "Googgle.com" (slide example) |
| **Reverse cybersquatting** ("reverse domain name hijacking") | A bad-faith party incorporates a company with the same name as an established site, then claims the real owner is the squatter and tries to take the domain through legal process | Setting up "Infinity Finance Ltd" to claim InfinityFinance.com |

⚠️ Slide 45 says "hackers" do this; more accurately, the bad actor is a *bad-faith claimant* who abuses the dispute rules. **[extra]** Domain disputes are commonly decided through arbitration policies such as ICANN's UDRP.

| Slide | Says | Better understanding |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | "stop compensating him" | Stop and compensate |
| 9 | Patent is a "license given to an author" | Exclusive right granted to an inventor |

| 14 vs 13 | Aspirin, Xerox as generic | Genericide vs always-generic terms |
| 31 | "legitimate transition" | Legitimate way |
| 35 | Chrome is open-source based | Chromium is; Chrome is not open source |
| 36 | Contact owner for proprietary licence | Depends on licence type; permissive licences don't require this |
| 39 | "criminal" | Usually civil infringer |
| 41 | Return domain to "copyright owner" | Trademark owner; usually sold for profit |

| Objectives | Mentions "designs" | No slide covers industrial designs |

**[extra, verified] Industrial designs:** Learning objective 1 lists "designs", but no slide covers them. In Malaysia, registered designs are protected for an initial 5 years, extendable by four further 5-year terms (maximum 25 years).

**Exam strategy:** use the slides' 15-year patent figure only if the question explicitly quotes the slide; otherwise state 20 years from filing.

## Practice

### A. Multiple choice

**A1.** Which is *not* registrable in Malaysia?
(a) Patent (b) Trademark (c) Trade secret (d) Industrial design

**A2.** KODAK is which kind of mark?
(a) Generic (b) Descriptive (c) Arbitrary (d) Fanciful/coined

**A3.** Under current Malaysian law, a patent lasts:
(a) 15 years from grant (b) 20 years from filing (c) Life + 50 years (d) 10 years renewable

**A4.** Registering "Googgle.com" to catch typing mistakes is:
(a) Name jacking (b) Typosquatting (c) Identity theft (d) Reverse cybersquatting

**A5.** In Malaysia copyright in a literary work generally lasts:
(a) 20 years from filing (b) Life of the author + 50 years (c) 10 years (d) Life + 70 years

### B. Short answer

**B1.** State the three tests for a patentable invention.
**B2.** Give three protective measures for trade secrets.
**B3.** Distinguish plagiarism from copyright infringement.

### C. Application

**C1.** Classify each mark on the spectrum: (i) BLACKBERRY for phones; (ii) COLD AND CREAMY for ice cream; (iii) CLOCK for clocks.
**C2.** A start-up's secret recipe is shared with a supplier. Which two protections would you use, and why is registration not an option?
**C3.** Someone registers "maybank-login-help.com" to collect passwords. Which cybersquatting type (slides 43–45) fits best, and which earlier chapter's attack does it feed?

### D. Thinking

**D1.** Compare protecting an invention by patent vs keeping it as a trade secret.
**D2.** A student copies a paragraph, adds a citation, but does not use quotation marks. Is it plagiarism, infringement or both? Discuss.

## Answers

**A1.** (c). Trade secrets can't be registered.
**A2.** (d).
**A3.** (b). The slide's 15-years-from-grant is out of date.
**A4.** (b).
**A5.** (b).

**B1.** New; involves an inventive step; industrially applicable.
**B2.** Any three of: strengthen employee agreements; access controls; address everyday practices; NDAs (external).
**B3.** Plagiarism is passing off someone else's words or ideas as your own (an ethical/academic offence). Copyright infringement is using a protected work without permission (a legal wrong). They can occur separately.

**C1.** (i) Arbitrary; (ii) Descriptive; (iii) Generic.
**C2.** NDA with the supplier (external) and access controls/employee agreements (internal). Registration is not available because trade secrets can't be registered in Malaysia; the value depends on keeping it confidential.
**C3.** Identity theft (impersonating a legitimate business); it feeds **phishing** (Chapter 2).

**D1.** Patent: public disclosure required, time-limited (20 years), costly and takes time, but gives exclusive rights enforceable against anyone. Trade secret: no registration or fixed term, no disclosure, but lost if it leaks or is independently discovered or reverse engineered (where lawful).
**D2.** Citing the source shows credit is given, but without quotation marks it presents someone's exact words as your own, which is still plagiarism as the slides describe it (cut-and-paste "without adequate attribution or quotation marks"). Whether it also infringes copyright depends on how much is copied and whether a legal exception applies; a short cited quote is often allowed. **[extra]**
